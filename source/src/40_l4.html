Conditional expectation

Conditional expectation function (CEF)

Discrete

$$\E[Y\mid X=x]=\sum_{y}y\,f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)$$

Continuous

$$\E[Y\mid X=x]=\int y\,f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)\,dy$$

The mean of the conditional distribution, which is still a distribution. As a function of the random variable $X$, $\E[Y\mid X]$ is itself random.

Slide 4: consumption given income

$$\E[\text{Consumption}\mid\text{Income}]=10+0.7\times\text{Income}$$

Expected consumption rises by 0.7 for each additional unit of income. This line is the object a regression estimates, which is where Chapter 2 goes next. Other slide examples: life expectancy given age and gender (slide 7), and predicted GPA given entrance exam scores.

Properties

$$\E[g(X)\mid X]=g(X)$$
$$\begin{gathered}\E[g(X)\,Y+h(X)\mid X]\\=g(X)\,\E[Y\mid X]+h(X)\end{gathered}$$

Anything that depends only on $X$ passes through like a constant, for example $\E[X^2\mid X]=X^2$. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\E[Y\mid X]=\E[Y]$.

Law of Iterated Expectations (LIE)

LIE

$$\E\big[\E[Y\mid X]\big]=\E[Y]$$

Tower property

$$\E\big[\E[Y\mid X,Z]\mid X\big]=\E[Y\mid X]$$

Average the group averages, weighted by group shares. Conditioning on less information means averaging away the extra conditioning variables.

Derivation (the slides show this through examples)

  1. Write the inner expectation with the conditional pdf.

    $$\E\big[\E[Y\mid X]\big]=\sum_x\Big(\sum_y y\,f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)\Big)f_X(x)$$

  2. Use $f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)$.

    $$=\sum_x\sum_y y\,f_{X,Y}(x,y)$$

  3. Sum out $x$ to get the marginal of $Y$.

    $$=\sum_y y\,f_Y(y)=\E[Y]$$

Examples

Relay runners (slide 9). Each of four legs is equally likely for a runner.

$$\begin{aligned}\E[\text{Time}]&=50(.25)+52(.25)\\&\quad+50(.25)+48(.25)=50\end{aligned}$$

Grades by section (slide 10). Sections hold 20%, 30%, 30% and 20% of students.

$$\begin{aligned}\E[\text{Grade}]&=70(.2)+80(.3)\\&\quad+90(.3)+95(.2)=84\end{aligned}$$

Mortality (slide 12). Here $Y$ is a mortality outcome, such as years of life remaining, $x$ is age, and M and F are male and female.

$$\begin{aligned} \E[Y\mid x]&=\E[Y\mid x,\mathrm{M}]\,\Pr\{\mathrm{M}\mid x\}\\ &\quad+\E[Y\mid x,\mathrm{F}]\,\Pr\{\mathrm{F}\mid x\} \end{aligned}$$

Degree and wage. The conditional means are $\E[Y\mid X=0]=20(.4)+40(.4)+60(.2)=36$ and $\E[Y\mid X=1]=20(.1)+40(.4)+60(.5)=48$, so a degree adds 12 dollars an hour on average here. Then

$$\E[Y]=.5(36)+.5(48)=42,$$

which matches the direct calculation from Lecture 3.

Sampling and estimators

The population has fixed parameters $\theta$, such as a mean or variance. You draw a random sample and use it to learn about $\theta$.

Random sample

$$Y_1,\dots,Y_n\ \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\ f_Y(y;\theta)$$

Independent: one draw tells you nothing about another. Identically distributed: every draw comes from the same population. Before sampling, each $Y_i$ is a random variable. After sampling, the data $y_i$ are numbers.

Slides 17-18 draw incomes from a lognormal population with mean 75 and standard deviation 25. Every sample gives a different histogram, and larger samples give a better one.

Unbiasedness

Unbiased estimator

$$\E[W]=\theta$$

Equivalently, the bias is zero

$$\Bias(W;\theta)=\E[W]-\theta=0$$
Derivation: the sample average is unbiased

  1. Start from the definition of the estimator.

    $$\E[\bar Y]=\E\Big[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i\Big]$$

  2. Apply linearity.

    $$=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E[Y_i]$$

  3. Every draw has mean $\mu$.

    $$=\frac1n(n\mu)=\mu\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Bias(\bar Y;\mu)=0$$

This holds for any value of $\mu$, so you know $\bar Y$ is unbiased even though you do not know $\mu$. Only linearity is used, not independence. On slide 25, 100 sample averages at $n=10$ average 75.26 against a true value of 75.

Sampling variance and standard error

Definitions

$$\var[W]=\E\big[(W-\E[W])^2\big]$$
$$\se[W]=\sqrt{\var[W]}$$

Here $W=h(Y_1,\dots,Y_n)$, so this is the variance of its sampling distribution. If $W$ is unbiased, $\E[W]=\theta$ and $\var[W]=\E[(W-\theta)^2]$.

Sample average

$$\var[\bar Y]=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$
$$\se[\bar Y]=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$$
Derivation

  1. Start from the definition.

    $$\var[\bar Y]=\var\Big[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i\Big]$$

  2. Pull out the constant, using $\var[aX]=a^2\var[X]$.

    $$=\frac{1}{n^2}\,\var\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i\Big]$$

  3. Independent draws have zero cross-covariances, so variances add.

    $$=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\var[Y_i]$$

  4. Every draw has variance $\sigma^2$.

    $$=\frac{1}{n^2}\,(n\sigma^2)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

The variance shrinks to zero as $n$ grows, which is why the sample average tightens around $\mu$. Halving the standard error takes four times the data. With the slides' population ($\sigma=25$):

Bias and variance are separate ideas

Unbiasedness is about the center of the sampling distribution. The standard error is about its spread. Using only $Y_1$ is also unbiased for $\mu$, but its variance is $\sigma^2$ no matter how large $n$ gets.

Sample variance and standard deviation

Sample variance

$$S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\bar Y)^2$$
$$S=\sqrt{S^2}$$

$S^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$. $S$ is not unbiased for $\sigma$, since the square root bends the average. The slides call the $n-1$ a degrees-of-freedom correction: estimating $\mu$ with $\bar Y$ uses up one parameter.

Derivation (added by me; the slides state the result)

  1. Write $Y_i-\bar Y=(Y_i-\mu)-(\bar Y-\mu)$, expand, and use $\sum(Y_i-\mu)=n(\bar Y-\mu)$.

    $$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\mu)^2-n(\bar Y-\mu)^2$$

  2. Take expectations. Each $\E[(Y_i-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$, and $\E[(\bar Y-\mu)^2]=\var[\bar Y]=\sigma^2/n$.

    $$\begin{aligned}\E\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\bar Y)^2\Big]&=n\sigma^2-n\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\\&=(n-1)\,\sigma^2\end{aligned}$$

  3. Divide by $n-1$.

    $$\E[S^2]=\sigma^2$$

Dividing by $n$ instead would give $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, which is 10% too small at $n=10$. In practice $\sigma$ is unknown, so the estimated standard error is $S/\sqrt n$. The slides stop before that step, but slide 28 shows $S$ settling near 25 as $n$ grows.
