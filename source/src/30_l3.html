Lecture 3: Summarizing distributions

Wooldridge Appendix B, pp 691-704

Full distributions are hard to compare and impossible to plot in more than two dimensions. Summary statistics, called moments when applied to a population, squeeze a distribution into a few numbers.

Expectation: where the distribution sits

Expected value

Discrete

$$\E[X]=\sum_{x\in\mathrm{Supp}(X)}x\,f_X(x)$$

Continuous

$$\E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,f_X(x)\,dx$$

The probability-weighted average outcome. It is the balancing point of the distribution and your best guess for $X$ if you know nothing else.

Expectation of a function

$$\E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)\,f_X(x)$$
$$\E[g(X,Y)]=\sum_x\sum_y g(x,y)\,f_{X,Y}(x,y)$$

Functions of two variables need the joint pdf.

Linearity

$$\E[c]=c$$
$$\E[aX+b]=a\,\E[X]+b$$
$$\E\Big[b+\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_jX_j\Big]=b+\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_j\,\E[X_j]$$

No independence needed. Expectation is an average, and averages are linear.

Common trap

$\E[X^2]\ne(\E[X])^2$. In general $\E[g(X)]\ne g(\E[X])$. Compute $\E[X^2]$ as $\sum x^2 f_X(x)$.

Variance: how spread out it is

Variance and standard deviation

$$\var[X]=\sigma_X^2=\E\big[(X-\mu_X)^2\big]=\E[X^2]-\mu_X^2$$
$$\sd[X]=\sigma_X=\sqrt{\var[X]}$$

Average squared distance from the mean. Units are X squared, so the standard deviation brings you back to the units of X.

Derivation of the shortcut $\E[X^2]-\mu^2$

  1. Expand the square.

    $$\E[(X-\mu)^2]=\E[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]$$

  2. Apply linearity.

    $$=\E[X^2]-2\mu\,\E[X]+\mu^2$$

  3. Use $\E[X]=\mu$.

    $$=\E[X^2]-2\mu^2+\mu^2=\E[X^2]-\mu^2$$

Scaling and shifting

$$\var[aX+b]=a^2\,\var[X]$$
$$\sd[aX+b]=|a|\,\sd[X]$$

Adding a constant does not change variability. Multiplying by $a$ multiplies variance by $a^2$. Only constants have zero variance. Proof: $aX+b-\E[aX+b]=a(X-\mu)$, then square and take expectations.

Standardizing (z-score)

$$Z=\frac{X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X}$$
$$\E[Z]=0,\qquad \var[Z]=1$$

Unitless, so you can compare variables measured in different units, like test scores or neural activation.

Covariance and correlation: how two variables move together

Covariance

$$\begin{aligned}\cov[X,Y]&=\E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]\\&=\E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y\end{aligned}$$

Also written $\sigma_{XY}$. Shared variation. Each joint outcome contributes the product of its deviations from the means, weighted by its probability. Outcomes in the upper right and lower left push covariance up, the other two push it down (slides 23-24).

Derivation of the shortcut $\E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y$

  1. Expand the product inside the expectation.

    $$\E\big[XY-\mu_Y X-\mu_X Y+\mu_X\mu_Y\big]$$

  2. Apply linearity and use $\E[X]=\mu_X$, $\E[Y]=\mu_Y$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&=\E[XY]-\mu_Y\mu_X-\mu_X\mu_Y+\mu_X\mu_Y\\&=\E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y\end{aligned}$$

Correlation

$$\begin{aligned}\corr[X,Y]=\rho_{XY}&=\frac{\cov[X,Y]}{\sd[X]\,\sd[Y]}\\[1ex]&=\cov\!\left[\frac{X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X},\,\frac{Y-\mu_Y}{\sigma_Y}\right]\end{aligned}$$

Unit-free: the covariance of the standardized variables.

Properties

  • $\cov[X,X]=\var[X]$
  • $\cov[a_1X+b_1,\ a_2Y+b_2]=a_1a_2\,\cov[X,Y]$
  • Cauchy-Schwarz: $|\cov[X,Y]|\le\sd[X]\,\sd[Y]$, so $-1\le\rho_{XY}\le1$
  • Independent implies $\cov[X,Y]=0$, because $\E[XY]=\sum_x\sum_y xy\,f_X(x)f_Y(y)=\E[X]\E[Y]$

Common trap

Independence implies zero covariance, but zero covariance does not imply independence. Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $Y=X^2$. Then $\cov[X,Y]=\E[X^3]-\E[X]\E[X^2]=0$, yet $Y$ is completely determined by $X$. Correlation only measures linear association.

Variance of sums

Two variables

$$\begin{aligned}\var[aX+bY]&=a^2\var[X]+b^2\var[Y]\\&\quad+2ab\,\cov[X,Y]\end{aligned}$$

The variability of a sum is each piece's variability plus their co-variability, counted twice.

Derivation

  1. Variance is a covariance with itself.

    $$\var[aX+bY]=\cov[aX+bY,\ aX+bY]$$

  2. Covariance is linear in each argument, so expand into four terms.

    $$\begin{aligned}&=a^2\cov[X,X]+ab\,\cov[X,Y]\\&\quad+ab\,\cov[Y,X]+b^2\cov[Y,Y]\end{aligned}$$

  3. Use $\cov[X,Y]=\cov[Y,X]$ and $\cov[X,X]=\var[X]$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&=a^2\var[X]+2ab\,\cov[X,Y]\\&\quad+b^2\var[Y]\end{aligned}$$

Many variables

$$\begin{aligned} \var\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_iX_i\Big]&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_i^2\var[X_i]\\ &\quad+2\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}a_ia_j\cov[X_i,X_j] \end{aligned}$$

If the $X_i$ are independent, or just uncorrelated, every cross term is zero and only the variance terms remain. This is the engine behind the standard error in Lecture 4.

Higher moments

Skew and kurtosis

$$\skew[X]=\E[Z^3]=\E\!\left[\frac{(X-\mu)^3}{\sigma^3}\right]$$
$$\kurt[X]=\E[Z^4]=\E\!\left[\frac{(X-\mu)^4}{\sigma^4}\right]$$

Symmetric distributions have skew 0. Normal distributions have kurtosis 3. The slides treat higher moments as minor, as long as they are finite.

Running example: degree and wage

$$\E[Y]=20(.25)+40(.40)+60(.35)=42$$
$$\E[X]=.5$$
$$\E[XY]=20(.05)+40(.20)+60(.25)=24$$
$$\cov[X,Y]=24-(.5)(42)=3$$
$$\var[Y]=\E[Y^2]-42^2=2000-1764=236$$
$$\sd[Y]=\sqrt{236}\approx15.36$$
$$\corr[X,Y]=\frac{3}{(.5)(15.36)}\approx0.39$$

A degree goes with higher wages, with a moderate positive correlation. Only the $X=1$ rows contribute to $\E[XY]$, since $X=0$ zeroes the product.
