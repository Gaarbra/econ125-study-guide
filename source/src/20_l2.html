Lecture 2: Modeling distributions

Wooldridge Appendix B, pp 684-691 and 704-710

A random variable $X$ measures a feature of an individual drawn from a population. A probability model gives the relative likelihood of each outcome, which tells you the expected outcome, how variable outcomes are, and which ones would be surprising. Think of a pdf as the histogram from a census of the whole population.

pdf and cdf

Probability density function (pdf)

$$f_X(x)=\Pr\{X=x\}$$

Discrete: sums to 1

$$f_X(x)\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{x\in\mathrm{Supp}(X)}f_X(x)=1$$

Continuous: integrates to 1

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1$$

For a continuous variable, $f_X$ is a density, not a probability. Probabilities are areas under it.

Cumulative distribution function (cdf)

$$F_X(x)=\Pr\{X\le x\}$$

Continuous

$$F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(s)\,ds$$

Discrete

$$F_X(x)=\sum_{s\le x}f_X(s)$$

Nondecreasing, and bounded between 0 and 1.

Computing probabilities from the cdf

$$\Pr\{X>x\}=1-F_X(x)$$

$$F_X^{-1}(q)=\min\{x\in\mathrm{Supp}(X):\Pr\{X\le x\}\ge q\}$$

Joint, marginal and conditional distributions

The joint distribution describes two variables at once, like household income and consumption. Marginals and conditionals are two different ways of reducing it.

Joint

$$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\Pr\{X=x,\ Y=y\}$$

Marginal: sum out the other variable

$$f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy$$
$$f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx$$

Use sums instead of integrals for discrete variables. Picture turning the joint histogram on its side and adding up the mass in each bin.

Conditional

$$f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}$$

The distribution of Y among the part of the population with $X=x$. It is a different function of $y$ for each $x$.

Derivation of the conditional pdf

  1. Take the slice of the joint histogram where $X=x$. It holds only the share $f_X(x)$ of the population, so it does not sum to 1.

    $$\sum_y f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)$$

  2. Rescale by the share of the population in the slice.

    $$\begin{aligned}\Pr\{Y=y\mid X=x\}&=\frac{\Pr\{X=x,\ Y=y\}}{\Pr\{X=x\}}\\&=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}\end{aligned}$$

  3. Check that it is a proper distribution.

    $$\sum_y f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f_X(x)}{f_X(x)}=1$$
NameNotationWhat it describes
Marginal$f_X(x),\ f_Y(y)$One variable across the whole population
Joint$f_{X,Y}(x,y)$Both variables together
Conditional$f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)$One variable with the other held at a value

Independence

Independence (each line implies the others)

$$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$$
$$f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f_X(x)f_Y(y)}{f_X(x)}=f_Y(y)$$
$$\Pr\{X=x,\ Y=y\}=\Pr\{X=x\}\Pr\{Y=y\}$$

Every slice of the joint histogram has the same shape, so knowing $X$ tells you nothing about $Y$.

Why it matters

  • Ceteris paribus on average. In a randomized trial, a coin flip assigns drug or placebo, so assignment is independent of everything else that affects the outcome.
  • Sampling. Independent draws each add new information, so information accumulates as n grows.

Where it fails: time series (effects persist), spatial or network samples, grouped treatments (a family that misses a flight together), and selected samples (people who sign up for a study share features the population does not).
