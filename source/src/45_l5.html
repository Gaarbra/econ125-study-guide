Lecture 5: Inference and hypothesis testing

Lecture 4 tells us how estimates vary across samples. Lecture 5 uses that sampling distribution to quantify uncertainty and test claims about the population.

For iid sampling, $\E[\bar Y]=\mu$ and $\var[\bar Y]=\sigma^2/n$ (slides 3-8). More data makes the mean more precise; it does not make individuals more alike. Revisit the Lecture 4 sampling simulation to see this distinction.

Consistency and the Law of Large Numbers

Consistency (slide 12)

$$W_n\xrightarrow{p}\theta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Pr\{|W_n-\theta|>\varepsilon\}\to0$$

For every fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the chance of missing the target by more than that amount goes to zero as the sample grows. We also write $\operatorname{plim}W_n=\theta$.

LLN and continuous mapping (slide 14)

$$\bar Y\xrightarrow{p}\mu,\qquad g(W_n)\xrightarrow{p}g(\theta)$$

The lecture assumes iid observations with finite variance for the LLN. The mapping $g$ must be continuous at the limit. Probability limits add and multiply; they divide when the denominator's limit is nonzero.

Derivation: sample variance and standard deviation are consistent (slide 16)

  1. Rewrite the sample variance.

    $$S^2=\frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY_i^2-\bar Y^2\right)$$

  2. Apply LLN to the first and second moments, then continuous mapping. The slide assumes a finite fourth moment to apply its finite-variance LLN to $Y_i^2$.

    $$S^2\xrightarrow{p}\E[Y^2]-\mu^2=\sigma^2$$

  3. Take the continuous square root.

    $$S\xrightarrow{p}\sigma$$

Unbiasedness and consistency answer different questions

Unbiasedness concerns the expectation at a given sample size. Consistency concerns concentration as $n$ grows. Using $Y_1$ alone is unbiased for $\mu$ but generally inconsistent. Dividing the sum of squared deviations by $n$ gives a biased yet consistent variance estimator.

Clarification of slide 13: bias tending to zero together with variance tending to zero is a sufficient condition for consistency, by mean-squared error and Chebyshev's inequality. These are not necessary conditions in full generality; convergence in probability alone does not guarantee convergence of moments.

Central Limit Theorem: concentration versus shape

CLT for an iid sample mean (slide 19)

$$\frac{\bar Y-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)$$
$$\bar Y\ \approx\ N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$

Assume $0<\sigma^2<\infty$. LLN says the mean concentrates around $\mu$. CLT describes the approximately normal shape of its standardized error. It does not say the individual observations become normal.

The lognormal income example has mean 75 and standard deviation 25. At $n=100$, the mean's standard error is 2.5; at $n=400$, it is 1.25. Quadrupling sample size halves the standard error. Slides 15 and 20 show both concentration and reduced skewness.

For $Z\sim N(0,1)$, write $\Phi(z)=\Pr\{Z\le z\}$. The upper-tail probability is $1-\Phi(z)$ (slide 21). Normal approximations can be poor in small, skewed samples (slide 10).

Confidence intervals

Approximate 95% interval for a population mean

$$\bar y\pm1.96\frac{s}{\sqrt n}$$

Slide 24 uses the convenient two-sigma rule, estimate $\pm2$ standard errors. The more precise standard-normal critical value is 1.96.

Derivation: turn a probability statement into an interval

  1. The central 95% of a standard normal lies between -1.96 and 1.96. Use the large-sample approximation with estimated standard error.

    $$\Pr\left\{-1.96\le\frac{\bar Y-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\le1.96\right\}\approx0.95$$

  2. Rearrange to isolate the population mean.

    $$\Pr\left\{\bar Y-1.96\frac{S}{\sqrt n}\le\mu\le\bar Y+1.96\frac{S}{\sqrt n}\right\}\approx0.95$$

Worked example (added): estimating household income

Suppose $n=100$, $\bar y=75$ and $s=25$, measured in thousands of dollars. The estimated standard error is $25/\sqrt{100}=2.5$.

$$75\pm1.96(2.5)=[70.10,79.90]$$

The slides' two-sigma shortcut gives $[70,80]$. These are intervals for the population mean, not intervals containing 95% of household incomes.

What 95% confidence means

Across repeated samples, about 95% of intervals made this way contain the fixed population mean. After observing the data, the interval either contains the mean or it does not. The estimate is always at its own interval's center; coverage concerns the unknown parameter (clarifying slides 24-25).

Hypothesis tests: null, alternative, statistic, decision

Slide 27 tests the claim $H_0:\mu=70$. Choose an alternative and significance level before examining the result: $H_1:\mu>70$, $H_1:\mu<70$, or $H_1:\mu\ne70$.

Test statistic (slide 29)

$$t=\frac{\bar y-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}$$

How many estimated standard errors separate your estimate from the null value? Under $H_0$, the statistic is approximately $N(0,1)$ in large samples.

Critical values above are rounded. The p-value is the probability, assuming the null is true, of a statistic at least as extreme in the direction(s) of the chosen alternative. Reject if $p\le\alpha$; otherwise fail to reject. A p-value is not the probability that the null is true, nor the probability your specific decision is mistaken.

Worked example continued: test a mean of 70

$$t=\frac{75-70}{2.5}=2$$

Two-sided: $p\approx0.0455$, so reject at 5% but not at 1%. Upper-sided: $p\approx0.0228$, so reject at 5%. Lower-sided: $p\approx0.9772$, so fail to reject; the estimate points in the opposite direction.

The 95% normal interval $[70.10,79.90]$ excludes 70, agreeing with the two-sided 5% test. Use matching critical values for this equivalence: the rounded two-sigma interval can differ at the boundary.

Errors, significance and power

DecisionNull is trueNull is false
RejectType I errorCorrect; probability is power
Fail to rejectCorrectType II error

The significance level $\alpha$ controls the rejection probability under the null (approximately for an asymptotic test). Power is $1-\beta$, where $\beta$ is the Type II error probability at a specified alternative. It depends on the true effect, sample size, variability, and test direction (slides 28, 31-32). Confidence level is $1-\alpha$, not $\alpha$.

For the slides' true mean of 75, a test of 70 gains power with larger samples in the upper-sided and two-sided cases. A lower-sided test looks in the wrong direction. Failing to reject never establishes that the null is true.

Finite-sample refinements

Slide 33 introduces Student's t and the bootstrap. With iid normally distributed observations and unknown variance, the sample-mean statistic follows an exact $t_{n-1}$ distribution under the null. Use its critical values and cdf instead of the normal ones. With nonnormal data, estimating the standard error alone does not make a small-sample t-test exact; the lecture's large-sample approximation still needs care. The bootstrap uses resampling to approximate a sampling distribution.

Check yourself: four quick questions
  1. Does LLN make household incomes normal? No. It makes the sample mean converge to the population mean; CLT concerns standardized sampling errors.
  2. If sample size rises from 100 to 400, what happens to standard error? It halves, holding population variability fixed.
  3. Does $p=0.04$ mean a 4% chance that the null is true? No. It is a tail probability computed under the null.
  4. Does a two-sided 95% interval containing the null imply rejection at 5%? No, for the matching test it implies failure to reject when the null is strictly inside the interval.
